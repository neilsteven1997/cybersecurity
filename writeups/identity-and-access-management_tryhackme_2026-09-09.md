# Identity and Access Management

---

Single Sign-On addresses the practical difficulty of managing numerous distinct usernames and passwords across systems. Users typically 
face separate credentials for logging into a workstation, checking email, and reaching a file share, a burden made worse by the sound 
practice of never reusing passwords. SSO replaces that multiplicity with a single set of login details: the user authenticates once to 
one system and thereby receives access to the other resources required for work.

Organisations gain several concrete benefits from this model. A single strong password is far more realistic for users to remember and 
protect than a collection of strong passwords. Multi-factor authentication needs to be configured and maintained only once rather than 
for every individual service. Support tasks such as password resets become simpler because they apply to one account. Daily efficiency
improves because users avoid repeated login prompts when moving between services.

Proper security still rests on a full set of processes that together safeguard confidentiality, integrity and availability. 
Identification, authentication, authorisation, access control, accountability and logging must all be present and correctly 
implemented; weakness in any one of them reduces the overall security of the systems involved. Strengthening a single process cannot 
compensate for gaps elsewhere.

The material examined the sequence of processes from identification through logging, along with access-control models and related 
standards for authentication and authorisation. Subsequent study can usefully continue with the rooms on Governance and Regulation, 
Threat Modelling or Risk Management.

---

### Key Takeaways
- One strong password is more practical for users than multiple strong passwords.
- Multi-factor authentication can be enabled and maintained once for the entire SSO environment.
- Password-reset and related support requests are confined to a single account.
- Users avoid repeated logins when accessing successive services.
- Security depends on complete processes covering identification, authentication, authorisation, access control, accountability and
- logging; any shortfall weakens the whole.
