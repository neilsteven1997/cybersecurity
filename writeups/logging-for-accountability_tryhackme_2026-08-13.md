# Logging for Accountability

--- 

Logging serves as a foundational record of network activity, establishing a reliable source of truth especially during incident 
response and security monitoring. It enables accountability by documenting actions that may later require attribution to specific 
users. Accountability forms the final element of the Identification, Authentication, Authorization, and Accountability model, 
which itself supports the protection of confidentiality, integrity, and availability of information. By holding users and systems 
responsible for their behavior, logging creates an auditable trail of events. For this accountability to remain effective the 
logs themselves must stay protected and demonstrably unaltered; any inability to verify original state undermines their value in 
both accountability and incident response.

I found the emphasis on authenticity particularly useful when reflecting on real investigations, because without proven integrity 
a log loses its force in non-repudiation. Non-repudiation ensures an individual cannot later deny an action, standing in contrast
to repudiation where an action is disputed, and it strengthens threat models such as STRIDE.

A Security Information and Event Management system collects, indexes, and searches data from endpoints and network locations. 
At a basic level it supplies real-time ingestion, alerting on anomalies, continuous monitoring, visualization of insights, and 
the ability to examine historical incidents. Common examples include Splunk, the ELK Stack, and QRadar. Architecture typically 
comprises a search head, an indexer, and a forwarder. For accountability the focus rests on the indexer and the ingestion paths 
that deliver data to it: agent or forwarder, port-forwarding, syslog, and direct upload. Storage presents equal risk; technical 
failures such as accidental index deletion or device corruption can destroy the record. Cold storage addresses long-term retention
needs by placing infrequently accessed data on low-cost media such as hard drives or tape, while hot storage keeps frequently 
used data on higher-performance solid-state or high-speed drives, with warm storage occupying intermediate tiers. Retention 
periods follow regulatory and organizational policy; an example sequence might keep data hot for six months, warm for three 
months, and cold for three years or longer. The Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard, for instance, requires audit logs
to be retained for one year and immediately available for ninety days.

Effective logs must contain the information needed for investigation. Manual sources include change logs composed by authors. 
Automated sources cover system and application logs generated by configuration or code. Additional sources required for 
compliance often include email and messaging records. Multiple complementary logs can cross-validate one another, strengthening
non-repudiation, yet excessive overlap creates noise and storage overhead. Correlation links separate log types to reconstruct a
complete incident narrative; a suspicious file-creation event might be explained by matching browser history or flagged by a 
preceding phishing email. Data enrichment further sharpens these relationships. In practice the choice of Security Information 
and Event Management features depends on whether the primary need is real-time monitoring or retrospective analysis.

A laboratory environment supplies a pre-loaded dataset inside a web application accessible after deploying the attached machine, 
allowing hands-on correlation of log sources to demonstrate accountability.

### Key Takeaways
- Understand where data originates, how it is stored, and how a security engineer can leverage it.
- Understand why accountability is important to security and how logging can help improve its efficacy.
- Apply logs and other data sources to incident response and the principle of accountability.
- Recommended prior knowledge includes logging capabilities and log data sources or completion of Intro to Logs, plus a basic
  understanding of Linux or completion of Linux Basics.
- Log sources can reveal files created, emails sent, and other tactics, techniques, and procedures outlined by the MITRE ATT&CK
  framework.
- SIEM benefits at the basic level include real-time log ingestion, alerting against abnormal activities, 24/7 monitoring and
  visibility, data insights and visualization, and ability to investigate past incidents.
- Types of data ingestion comprise agent/forwarder, port-forwarding, syslog, and upload.
- Manual log sources are any log manually written or composed by an author, such as change logs.
- Automated log sources are any logs automatically generated by default from configuration, tool, or developer, such as system
  logs and application logs.
- Other types of logs required for compliance include email logs and messaging or other communication.
